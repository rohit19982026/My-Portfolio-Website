## What I walked into

The architecture call was already made. The team was assembled. I came in at SOW signature on a fixed-price engagement with the cutover date already in the deck.

What nobody had written down was this: the senior data engineer on the client side had built the Redshift estate over six years. She knew every object in it. She hadn't been the decision-maker when the migration was approved. And she wasn't fully convinced it was a good idea.

She was also going to be the gatekeeper on every access grant, every legacy-table question, and every UAT sign-off.

That's the project I actually picked up.

## The technical PM work

Setup in week one was clean — budget and roles checked against the SOW, a second PM's eyes on the plan before anything went external, access requests out the same day as kickoff. On a migration, access is always the first thing to block the team.

Weekly cadence ran the same way every week: standups, sprint planning, review/retro, status report to the account team and client sponsor on the same day. RAID log kept current. Color status held honest — I flipped to yellow twice on access delays the client side owned, and escalated one stall to the client sponsor and our account lead when it crossed three weeks open.

The cutover worked with the architect and the client engineering team: comms timeline, rollback window, downstream sign-offs. Closure ran the standard way out — final invoicing reconciled, NPS sent, KT to the team taking the platform forward, access removed.

That's the PM work. It's not the interesting part of this engagement.

## The actual work

Week two, I asked the architect for an hour to understand which Redshift objects the client's data lead had personally built. The answer: most of the ones the marketing team still ran their weekly reporting off.

I moved that tranche from sprint 2 to sprint 6. The architect pushed back — it made the dependency graph harder. I held the line. If we migrated those objects early and something was wrong, the person who'd flag it loudest was the same person I needed signing off UAT. Easier to let her validate them herself, with her own hands, close to the cutover.

In week three, I restructured the weekly client meeting so the data lead spoke first, not the sponsor. Small thing. Cost the sponsor maybe 90 seconds of airtime. By week four, she was saying "we" instead of "you."

In week nine, a senior stakeholder at the client asked in a hallway chat whether we could add a dashboard that wasn't in the SOW. I got the architect's estimate in writing, brought it to the AE, and routed it through change-order. Lost a small amount of goodwill that week. Protected the margin for the rest of the engagement.

## What the engagement actually required

On the surface: PM a two-region engineering pod across a fixed-price engagement. Ship the migration on time.

Under that: earn the trust of someone who'd spent six years building the thing we were replacing. The technical work was the engineering team's. My job was making sure the one person who could quietly veto the UAT instead decided to co-own the outcome.

She signed off UAT. The migration shipped. That's what I'm most satisfied with from this one.

## What I'd do differently

I assumed the client sponsor's sign-off meant the data lead was aligned. That took me three weeks to disprove.

Week five ended up being a harder conversation than it needed to be — one that week one could have absorbed easily. I've stopped assuming a manager's alignment transfers to the senior ICs who've been in a role long enough to have opinions that weren't asked for.
