## What two years of T&M looks like from the PM side

On a long T&M engagement, the PM job is not the same as on a fixed-price sprint. There's no cutover date driving urgency. The thing that gets you through two years — and to a renewal, and then another — is showing up the same way every week until it would feel strange to go somewhere else.

That's the frame for this engagement. A multi-year build on MWAA, dbt, and Snowflake for a marketing tech company. The engineering and architect team owned the platform. I owned the cadence that held the relationship together across the full window.

## How it ran

The Friday status note went out every week for two years. Same day, same format, same level of detail. The sponsor got three lines and a number. The product owner got the blocker list. The senior client engineer — more on them in a moment — got proof we'd actually read their previous comments before writing new ones.

I tracked burn against the SOW every Friday, reconciled against the PMO's own reporting so my numbers and leadership's matched exactly when renewal conversations came around. They always did.

Two scope-growth signals the client mentioned in passing turned into formal change-orders rather than free work absorbed into the burn. One hallway request I turned into an estimate, sized with the AE, and offered formally — it either went in officially or it didn't. The engagement was renewed twice. I don't think the financial discipline was incidental to that.

There was a hard week-fourteen conversation with the client's product owner about a six-week access stall. I ran it not as a complaint but as a clean ownership statement — here's what's blocked, here's what we need, here's who needs to act. Uncomfortable in the moment. Cleared a recurring pattern for the rest of the engagement.

## The stakeholder I read wrong

Three weeks into the engagement, the client's senior data engineer decided they didn't trust consultants. They'd been in their role six years, had survived three platform migrations, and had a standing theory that outside teams made their life worse and then left.

That's a real concern. I took it seriously and spent about two months trying to address it directly — extra context in writing, extra time in 1:1s, accommodating their PRs even when it cost us a sprint of velocity.

They were never going to be brought around. The shape of their role didn't allow it. Their value to their organization came partly from being the person who kept consultants honest. Winning them over would have undercut the function they were there to perform.

What I should have done: accepted that in the first month and routed the work around them. That's what I did in year two, once it was obvious. The engagement got measurably easier.

The cost of the first approach wasn't just the velocity. It was the slow erosion of my own credibility with our team, who could see I was burning energy on something that wasn't going to move.

## What the status note actually did

When the renewal conversations came around — twice — the AE had two years of weekly status history to walk into the room with. The context was already there. The hard conversations didn't need to be reconstructed from memory; they were in writing, with dates and resolutions.

The note wasn't just about information. It was the artifact that proved consistency over a long window. That's a different job than a kickoff deck or a closure report, and it's underrated.

## What I'd do differently

Default to triaging stakeholder investment earlier than feels fair. Two months is too long to try to win someone over when their role structure makes the outcome improbable. The right signal isn't the person's attitude — it's whether winning them over is even available as an outcome given how their organization works.

I'd ask that question in week two now, not month two.
