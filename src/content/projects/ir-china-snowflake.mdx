## What this engagement was

A six-week Snowflake build for a global industrial manufacturer's China factory operations. Three timezones: US delivery lead, India-based engineers, China-site client team. Ten and a half hours between the US and China.

People assume that's the hard part. It wasn't.

## How the PM work ran

Setup in week one was the same as any engagement: SOW read, financials tracked, second PM's review of the plan before anything went external. Kickoff scheduled at a time that actually worked across all three regions — China joined first thing in their day, the US lead late afternoon theirs. Access requests out on day one.

The schedule re-baselining around Lunar New Year happened in week three. That conversation went well. I'd walked the China-site product owner through it in a 1:1 first, then the sponsor, then the leadership meeting. It was a significant reshaping of the timeline — front-loading vendor development, back-loading client validation, protecting the critical path across the holiday. It landed as a co-decision because the right person heard it before the room did.

Closure ran clean. Final status, NPS, KT to the ongoing support team, access removed.

## What actually happened in week two

By the end of week one it was clear the live weekly leadership meeting wasn't going to hold. The China-site team's schedules didn't reliably overlap with the US lead's. Decision lag was already building, and we were only a week in.

Async-first was the right answer: written status at a fixed point in each region's day, a shared decision register instead of a meeting dependency, the live call moved to fortnightly. This is how you run a three-timezone engagement and actually get decisions made.

I announced it in the week-two leadership meeting. The China-site product owner heard it for the first time in front of their leadership and ours.

They said all the right things. Their face said something else.

They were on board — the cadence change was the correct call and they knew it. But I'd changed how their team interfaced with us without asking them first. Fifteen minutes of pre-conversation before the meeting would have made it a co-decision instead of a heads-up.

## Week two vs. week three

The contrast is the thing I've thought about most from this engagement.

Two structural decisions, same week. Same magnitude of impact on the project.

Week two: made the right call the wrong way. Announced in the group before the key stakeholder had been consulted privately. The cadence worked. Trust took the rest of the engagement to rebuild.

Week three: 1:1 with the China-site PO first, then the sponsor, then the meeting. Same announcement landed as a shared decision rather than something being done to them.

The lesson isn't "do 1:1s first." It's more specific: anyone whose working rhythm is changing has to be the second person in the room to know, not the eighth. Especially when that person's default mode is to defer in groups and disagree in private.

## What I'd do differently

Most of the answer is already above.

The one thing I'd add: I noticed in week one that the China-site product owner consistently spoke last in group meetings, and I'd filed that away without acting on it. That was the signal that the async announcement needed a different approach. I had the data; I didn't use it in time.
